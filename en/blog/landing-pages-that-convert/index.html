Landing pages that turn ad clicks into customers

By Waled Abu Oweida
October 11, 2026 · 7 min read

You're paying for ads, and people are clicking… yet the messages are few and the sales fewer. In most cases I see, the problem isn't the ad itself — it's where the ad sends people. Here's what a landing page is and how to build one that turns a click into a customer, based on my work with clients in Egypt, Libya and the Gulf.

What is a landing page, and how is it different from your home page?

Your home page is like a shop window: it introduces you, lists all your services, and has a menu, a blog and plenty of links. That's great for someone getting to know you. A landing page is something else entirely: a page built for one purpose only.

  • One goal: the visitor messages you on WhatsApp, fills in a form, calls, or buys.
  • One offer: a specific service, product or package, not everything you do.
  • No distractions: no full menu and no links that lead the visitor away.

Why sending ad traffic to your home page or profile wastes money

When someone clicks an ad for "teeth whitening at a special price", that's exactly what they came for. If they land on a home page with ten services, or a Facebook profile full of old posts, they'll look around, and if they don't quickly find what they came for, they leave. You've paid for that click for nothing. Every extra step between the click and the contact loses you people, and a landing page shortens that path as much as possible.

Anatomy of a landing page that sells

The elements I put into every landing page, roughly in this order:

  1. A headline that matches the ad: the same promise and the same words, so the visitor instantly knows they're in the right place.
  2. A sub-headline: a line or two that clarifies the offer: who it's for and what it solves.
  3. A hero image or video: showing the product, the result, or you personally, not an unrelated stock photo.
  4. A clear offer: exactly what they get, and the price or how to start, without beating around the bush.
  5. Benefits, not features: instead of "latest laser device", say "a faster, more comfortable session". People buy outcomes.
  6. Social proof: genuine client reviews, logos of companies you've worked with, and numbers you can back up.
  7. Answers to objections: an FAQ section that handles the "but what if…?" before the visitor has to ask.
  8. A guarantee or risk reversal: a free consultation, a trial, a refund option or cash on delivery, depending on your business and what you can genuinely commit to.
  9. One repeated call to action: the same action (WhatsApp, form or call) at the top, in the middle and at the end.
Tip: Any numbers on the page must be real and provable. An inflated figure might win a click, but it breaks trust the moment the customer discovers the truth.

Urgency, used honestly

"Offer ends this month" or "only 10 spots available" help people decide, as long as they're true. A countdown that resets on every refresh, or an offer that "ends today" every day, is easy to spot and hurts your reputation more than it helps.

Forms: the shorter, the better

Every extra field is another reason for the visitor to give up. In most cases a name and mobile number are enough; you can learn the rest on the call or on WhatsApp. And if your audience lives on WhatsApp, as much of the Arab market does, a WhatsApp button with a pre-filled message is far easier than a long form.

Mobile first, and speed above all

Most people who click social media ads are on their phones, often on a connection that isn't always fast. That's why I design the page for mobile first:

  • A light page: compressed images and no heavy add-ons it doesn't need.
  • A clear button within thumb's reach: large, easy to tap, and ideally visible as you scroll.
  • Concise copy: short paragraphs and clear headings that can be scanned quickly.

A slow page gets closed before anyone sees your offer, and you've still paid for the click.

Speak to each audience in its own language

A customer in Cairo is not the same as one in Tripoli, Riyadh or Dubai. Dialect, currency, payment methods and even the kind of examples you use all matter. If you advertise in more than one country, make a version of the page for each audience: the headline in their dialect, prices in their currency, and the right contact number. This is called message match, and it's one of the things that makes the biggest difference to results.

Track the page: without data you're guessing

A landing page should tell you whether it's working:

  • Ad pixels: such as the Meta Pixel and the Google tag, installed correctly on the page.
  • Clear events: record WhatsApp clicks, calls and form submissions as separate events.
  • A thank-you page: after the form is submitted, send visitors to a thank-you page. It reassures them their request arrived and gives you an accurate way to count conversions.

Fed this data, the ad platform learns who actually gets in touch and finds you more people like them.

Test before you judge

Don't rely on gut feeling. Run an A/B test: two versions of the page with one difference, and let the numbers decide. The things most worth testing:

  • The headline: lead with the price or with the result?
  • The offer: a discount, a gift or a free consultation?
  • Form length: two fields or four? WhatsApp or a form?

Change one thing at a time, and give each test enough time before deciding.

Quick examples by business type

  • Clinic: a page for a single treatment, featuring the doctor and their experience, patient reviews shared with consent, and a WhatsApp booking button.
  • Restaurant: a specific offer or meal, real photos of the food, the delivery area, and a direct order button.
  • Real estate: one page per project: location, unit sizes, payment plans, and a short form to request details.
  • Online store: one page per product with plenty of photos, sizes, payment and shipping options, and the return policy.
  • Services: the problem you solve, how you work step by step, samples of your work, and a "request a consultation" button.

Common mistakes I see all the time

  • Sending every ad to the same page regardless of the offer or audience.
  • A headline that doesn't match the ad, so visitors feel they've landed in the wrong place.
  • Several buttons with different goals, so the visitor hesitates and does nothing.
  • A page that looks great on desktop but is slow or broken on mobile.
  • Reviews that look fake, or exaggerated numbers.
  • No tracking, so nobody knows which ads bring customers and which just burn budget.

The bottom line

Ads bring you the visit, but the landing page closes the sale. One page with a clear goal, an understandable offer, genuine proof and an easy button can change what the same ad budget achieves.
